Mars Avatar Project

Control Without Presence

Mars Avatar Project is a reference architecture for supervising fleets of autonomous robotic avatars operating under full Earth–Mars communication latency.

The one-way signal delay of 4 to 22 minutes is treated as a design condition, not an obstacle. The operator on Earth transmits goals; onboard autonomy determines how those goals are achieved. This site is the public reference for the concept and its published documentation.

Figure 1. One-way light-travel time, Earth to Mars, across the synodic cycle. Round-trip command latency is 8 to 44 minutes. Continuous teleoperation is therefore not available at any point in the mission, and the architecture is specified accordingly.

§ 01

Project overview

The Mars Avatar Project is a concept study of a telepresence architecture for remote exploration of Mars, in which humans retain full intellectual control from Earth while a fleet of specialised robotic avatars provides physical presence on the surface.

The problem

A robot on Mars cannot be driven from Earth. A radio signal takes 4 to 22 minutes to arrive and as long again to return. By the time an obstacle appears on the operator's screen, the vehicle passed it many minutes ago. Crews have driven rovers remotely before, but only where the signal took under a second. No reaction time survives the Mars gap.

Current rovers already drive themselves: onboard autonomy evaluated most of Perseverance's first-year traverse. But each vehicle is a single general-purpose machine following a route planned days in advance, and each new decision waits for the next command cycle from Earth. The principle — people set goals, machines execute them — has been established since Sheridan's work on supervisory control. The system integration has not been done.

The approach

Earth transmits the objective: reach this location, acquire that sample, avoid this hazard. A fleet on Mars determines the method: it plans routes, responds to hazards within seconds, reallocates work among units, and reports against the objective when the next window opens. Every decision of consequence remains with the operator. What the operator gives up is the steering, not the authority.

Why it matters

Site preparation for a crewed landing — pads, power, stored consumables — must begin years before anyone arrives, with no one present to supervise it directly. After landing, crew time is the scarcest resource on the surface and is poorly spent on routine tasks. Between the two lies the science itself, which requires attention daily rather than once per command cycle.

Control without presence is the missing layer between current rovers and permanent surface operations. Solved once at this distance, the same architecture applies to the lunar far side, to asteroids, and to the outer moons. The architecture matters more than the destination.

Most individual components of such a system have already been demonstrated in isolation: goal-based commanding on NASA rovers (AutoNav, AEGIS), supervisory telepresence from orbit (ESA METERON), and immersive avatar interfaces (ANA Avatar XPRIZE). The contribution of this work is their system integration into a single coordination layer, designated Mission AI, specified as a five-layer reference architecture with a formal goal-package protocol, graduated decision classes, and measurable quality metrics. The task-allocation layer builds on decentralised market-based methods in the CBBA lineage.

The central claim is deliberately narrow and falsifiable: a coordinating fleet does not remove the boundary of the modelled fault space, but it converts a class of mission-loss risks into throughput losses, while leaving correlated (common-mode) failures untouched.

Key contributions

  1. C-1

    Supervisory architecture

    Five layers, from operator intent to actuator command, with a formal goal-package protocol and graduated decision classes that define what the fleet may decide without consultation.

  2. C-2

    Mission AI coordination layer

    The principal original contribution: a shared world model, task decomposition, allocation, execution monitoring, and fault management for a heterogeneous fleet under delay.

  3. C-3

    Multi-robot coordination

    Decentralised, market-based task allocation extended to intermittent communication windows and partially degraded fleets.

  4. C-4

    Human strategic oversight

    Human-in-command rather than human-in-the-loop. The operator sets objectives, constraints, and acceptance criteria; the fleet reports against them.

  5. C-5

    Earth–Mars latency architecture

    A 4 to 22 minute one-way delay treated as a fixed design condition, with communication scheduling and degradation hierarchy derived from it.

  6. C-6

    Open research framework

    The full specification, its assumptions, and its open problems are published under CC BY 4.0 and archived with a persistent identifier, so that the claims can be examined and contested.

§ 02

Scope

The boundaries of this work are stated before its content, so that no claim is read more broadly than it is meant.

What this is

  • A reference architecture — a specification of structure, interfaces, and decision authority.
  • A research concept, developed and documented as a technical study.
  • An open publication, archived with a DOI and licensed CC BY 4.0.
  • Independent work, carried out without institutional affiliation or funding.
  • An invitation for peer review, criticism, and technical collaboration.

What this is not

  • Not flight hardware. No component described here has been built.
  • Not operational software. No implementation is offered or implied.
  • Not a NASA, ESA, or other agency mission, and not endorsed by any agency.
  • Not a commercial product, and not a solicitation of investment.
  • Not a validated engineering system. Nothing here has been experimentally verified.

§ 04

Architecture and Mission AI

Authority flows in one direction and evidence flows back in the other. Between them sits the layer that makes delayed supervision tractable.

  1. Stage 01 Human operator Defines objectives, constraints, priorities, and acceptance criteria. Retains authority over irreversible actions.
  2. Stage 02 Mission AI Translates goals into tasks, allocates them across the fleet, monitors execution, and handles faults within its decision class.
  3. Stage 03 Robotic fleet Executes tasks on the surface, negotiates local reallocation, and reports state against the goal package.
  4. Stage 04 Scientific results Measurements, samples, and constructed assets, returned with provenance and the decision record that produced them.

Return path: telemetry, deviation reports, and decision records are transmitted to Earth on the same delayed channel. The operator reviews outcomes; the operator does not steer.

Operational layers

The same system, viewed by where each function physically resides. Every layer is designed around one constraint: the two planets cannot hold a conversation.

  • L-1

    Earth command layer

    The mission team defines objectives, constraints, and safety limits. There is no moment-to-moment piloting: the team reviews returned results and authorises the next goal package as the communication schedule allows.

  • L-2

    Relay and scheduling layer

    The link between the planets. Outbound commands and inbound telemetry are queued, prioritised by information value, and transmitted when a window opens.

  • L-3

    Avatar autonomy layer

    Onboard each avatar, local autonomy converts an assigned task into action — route planning, tool operation, hazard response — within seconds and without reference to Earth.

Mission AI

Mission AI is the principal original contribution of the white paper. It is specified as a coordination layer rather than a controller: it holds the fleet's shared understanding of the mission and decides how work is distributed when the operator cannot be consulted in time. It does not set mission priorities. Which site to investigate and which sample to acquire remain decisions of the team on Earth.

  • Mission coordinationMaintains the active goal package and the ordering of objectives across the fleet.
  • Shared world modelA single reconciled representation of terrain, assets, and fleet state, updated from all units.
  • Task decomposition and allocationBreaks objectives into executable tasks and assigns them by decentralised market-based bidding.
  • Fleet synchronisationKeeps unit-level plans mutually consistent when units act on partial information.
  • Execution monitoringCompares observed progress against expected progress and raises deviations early.
  • Fault managementIsolates failed units, reassigns their tasks, and preserves the mission where the fault is within the modelled space.
  • Degradation hierarchyA defined order in which capability is surrendered, so that partial capability is never traded for total loss.
  • Communication schedulingAllocates limited uplink and downlink windows by information value, not by request order.

§ 06

Robotic fleet

Version 3.6 describes six notional unit classes. They are specified by role and by the demands each places on the coordination layer, not as hardware designs.

  • UNIT CLASS SCI-1

    Scientific rover

    Long-range traverse, in-situ measurement, and sample acquisition. Drives the shared world model with the widest area coverage of any unit.

  • UNIT CLASS CON-1

    Construction robot

    Site preparation, assembly, and emplacement of surface infrastructure. Tasks are long, sequential, and costly to abandon mid-execution.

  • UNIT CLASS CRG-1

    Cargo robot

    Transport of samples, components, and consumables between sites. The primary coupling between otherwise independent work areas.

  • UNIT CLASS MNT-1

    Maintenance robot

    Inspection, servicing, and recovery of other units. Its availability is the main determinant of how far the degradation hierarchy is exercised.

  • UNIT CLASS LAB-1

    Laboratory unit

    On-site analysis of acquired samples. Converts raw material into information that can be prioritised for the limited downlink budget.

  • UNIT CLASS REC-1

    Reconnaissance drone

    Aerial survey ahead of surface operations. Reduces traverse risk by refreshing the world model faster than ground units can.

§ 07

Research context and roadmap

The architecture is assembled from results that already exist. The table states what each contributed and precisely where this work extends it.

Table 1. Prior work and the extension proposed in White Paper v3.6.
Previous work Established contribution Mars Avatar extension
Sheridan (1992), supervisory control The theoretical basis for humans working through semi-autonomous machines rather than steering them directly. Supervisory control applied to a heterogeneous fleet, with decision authority made explicit as graduated decision classes.
NASA Surface Telerobotics (2013) Crew aboard the ISS supervised the K10 rover by command sequencing rather than joystick control. The same command discipline extended from sub-second delay to the full interplanetary case, with no low-latency fallback.
ESA METERON / Analog-1 (2019–2022) Astronauts operated the Interact rover from orbit and in analogue terrain under injected delay, with force feedback. Supervision specified where force feedback is unavailable in principle, and the interface reports state rather than transmitting motion.
Perseverance autonomy — AutoNav, ENav, AEGIS Flight-proven onboard autonomy: AutoNav evaluated most of the rover's first-Mars-year traverse, and AEGIS selects science targets onboard. Goal packages issued to many units at once, with allocation decided after transmission rather than planned on Earth.
Market-based task allocation (CBBA and successors) Decentralised, provably convergent assignment among cooperating agents. Allocation under intermittent communication windows and a partially degraded fleet.

Path forward

The concept is published and open. The phases below state where the work can go next. Phases 2 to 4 are open for collaborators; none is funded, scheduled, or under way. The white paper places these within a longer verification sequence — Earth, then Moon, then Mars — with explicit transition criteria between stages.

Known limitations and assumptions

  • Coordination does not extend the boundary of the modelled fault space; it only changes the consequence of faults inside it.
  • Correlated and common-mode failures are explicitly not addressed by the architecture.
  • Verification of learned components remains an open problem and is not solved here.
  • The simulation-to-reality gap is assumed, not quantified.
  • Detection of the unmodelled case is stated as an open research problem.
  • Cost figures in the white paper are order-of-magnitude estimates, not engineering estimates.
  • The document is single-author and has not undergone formal peer review.
