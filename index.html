Mars Avatar Project

Control Without Presence

A reference architecture for supervising fleets of autonomous robotic avatars under full Earth–Mars communication latency.

An open research concept proposing how human operators on Earth can remain meaningfully in charge of scientific and engineering work on the Martian surface without real-time teleoperation. Humans define goals and constraints; onboard autonomy executes them.

About the Project

The Problem

You cannot drive a robot on Mars from Earth. A radio signal takes 4 to 24 minutes to arrive — and the same time to return. By the time a rock appears on a screen on Earth, the rover encountered it many minutes earlier. Astronauts have remotely driven rovers before, but only when the signal delay was less than one second. No human reaction time survives the Mars gap.

Today’s Mars rovers already drive themselves for much of their traverse. Perseverance covers most of its route without continuous human oversight. Yet each rover remains a single, general-purpose machine following routes planned days ahead. Every new high-level decision waits for the next command cycle from Earth. The long-recognized principle is clear: humans should set goals; machines should execute. A complete system that applies this principle to a coordinated fleet under full interplanetary latency has not yet been assembled.

The Solution

The Mars Avatar Project is a reference architecture for that system. The core problem is not locomotion of a single robot; it is keeping human operators meaningfully responsible for work they cannot observe in real time and cannot steer by hand.

Earth transmits the what: scientific objectives, constraints, and safety limits. A coordinated fleet of specialized robotic avatars on Mars determines the how: local path planning, hazard response, mutual support, and task completion reporting. Operators retain authority over every strategic decision; they no longer issue individual motion commands.

Why It Matters

Before the first human landing, surface infrastructure must be prepared: landing pads, power systems, and consumable stockpiles. That work begins years earlier, with no crew present. After landing, astronaut time is both limited and high-risk for routine tasks that supervised robots can perform. Continuous science operations — sampling, surveys, instrument tending — require daily attention that exceeds the capacity of discrete command windows.

Control without presence is the missing architectural layer between current single-rover autonomy and future sustained surface operations. The same supervisory approach applies to the lunar far side, asteroids, and the outer-planet moons — any destination where real-time teleoperation is impossible.

Scope of the Claim

What the project claims

  • No existing system coordinates a heterogeneous robotic fleet under full interplanetary latency (4–24 min one-way).
  • Mission AI is proposed as the missing central coordination layer.
  • The architecture integrates goal-based commanding, supervisory control, and multi-robot coordination into a coherent reference design.
  • Components already demonstrated in isolation (AutoNav, AEGIS, METERON, etc.) can be composed under the proposed structure.

What the project does not claim

  • AI does not solve unforeseen or unmodeled failures.
  • The architecture does not replace future crewed missions.
  • Economic figures, if present, are illustrative only and are not mission budgets.
  • Neural interfaces are not required.
  • No flight hardware or operational system is claimed to exist.

This is a conceptual research architecture proposal. It is independent, open, and intended for scientific discussion and further development.

System Architecture

The reference architecture is organized as a vertical control hierarchy designed around the hard constraint of non-real-time communication between Earth and Mars.

01

Human Operator

Mission scientists and operators on Earth define high-level goals, scientific priorities, operational constraints, and safety boundaries. They review returned results and authorize subsequent goal packages according to the communication schedule. There is no continuous teleoperation.

↓
02

Mission AI

The principal original contribution of the project. Mission AI is the central coordination layer resident with the robotic fleet. It interprets goal packages, maintains a shared world model, allocates tasks across the fleet, monitors execution, and applies a degradation hierarchy when resources or conditions degrade. Strategic intent remains with humans; tactical coordination is performed locally.

↓
03

Robotic Fleet

A heterogeneous set of specialized robotic units that execute allocated tasks. Individual vehicles retain local autonomy for hazard avoidance, path following, and tool operation. Coordination and re-planning occur through Mission AI rather than through direct Earth commanding of each unit.

↓
04

Scientific Results

Data products, samples, infrastructure status, and status reports are packaged and returned during communication windows. Operators on Earth evaluate outcomes and issue the next set of goals.

Mission AI

Mission AI is the principal original contribution of the Mars Avatar Project. It is the onboard coordination layer that enables a fleet of specialized robots to operate coherently under interplanetary latency while remaining under human supervisory control.

Fleet Coordinator

Interprets goal packages received from Earth and decomposes them into executable task sets distributed across available robotic units according to capability, location, energy state, and current workload.

Shared World Model

Maintains a consistent, multi-agent representation of terrain, hazards, resource locations, and asset status. The model is updated by all units and used for planning and conflict resolution.

Task Allocation

Assigns and, when necessary, reassigns work among the fleet. Auction-based or capability-matching mechanisms are proposed for simulation and evaluation.

Execution Monitoring

Tracks progress of allocated tasks, detects deviations or anomalies within the limits of the onboard models, and reports status at the next communication opportunity.

Degradation Hierarchy

Defines ordered responses when communication is lost, energy is constrained, or units fail. The hierarchy preserves safety and scientific return under progressive loss of capability.

Mission AI does not set mission priorities or scientific objectives. Those remain the exclusive responsibility of the human operators on Earth. The avatar fleet is trusted to execute safely and report accurately; it is not authorized to redefine the mission.

Robotic Fleet

Rather than a single general-purpose rover, the architecture proposes a small, heterogeneous fleet of specialized units that coordinate under Mission AI on shared objectives.

Scientific Rover

Primary mobile science platform for sample acquisition, in-situ analysis support, and instrument deployment across extended traverses.

Construction Robot

Capable of preparing landing pads, assembling modular structures, and performing surface infrastructure tasks required before crew arrival.

Cargo Robot

Transport of samples, spare parts, consumables, and equipment between units and fixed infrastructure nodes.

Maintenance Robot

Inspection, repair, and servicing of other fleet members and surface assets to extend operational lifetime.

Laboratory Unit

Stationary or semi-mobile analytical facility for sample processing, storage, and detailed measurement beyond the capacity of mobile platforms.

Reconnaissance Drone

Aerial or elevated platform for rapid terrain mapping, site survey, and extension of local situational awareness ahead of ground units.

Illustrations, if present, are concept sketches only. No flight hardware has been constructed. The fleet composition is a functional reference design, not a flight configuration.

Scientific Context

The Mars Avatar Project builds on three decades of research in supervisory control, crew-operated telerobotics, and flight-proven rover autonomy.

  • Sheridan, T.B. (1992). Telerobotics, Automation, and Human Supervisory Control. MIT Press. The foundational text on supervisory control — humans working through semi-autonomous machines rather than steering them directly. The theoretical basis for the operator-to-supervisor shift at the core of this project. MIT Press →
  • NASA Surface Telerobotics (2013). Astronauts aboard the ISS remotely supervised the K10 rover at NASA Ames across four simulated mission phases — the first fully interactive operation of a planetary rover by a crew member in space, using command sequencing rather than joystick control. Paper (arXiv) →
  • ESA METERON / Analog-1 (2019–2022). A multi-year ESA campaign in which astronauts operated the Interact rover from orbit and in lunar-analog terrain on Mount Etna, testing force-feedback control under injected signal delay. ESA →
  • Verma et al. (2023). Autonomous robotics is driving Perseverance rover’s progress on Mars. Science Robotics. Flight-proven evidence that onboard autonomy works at Mars: AutoNav evaluated 88 % of the rover’s first-Mars-year traverse, including record drives without human review. Science Robotics →
  • Enhanced Autonomous Navigation (ENav). The driving algorithm behind Perseverance’s autonomy: two-stage path selection and hazard-aware collision checking on severely limited onboard compute — a template for local autonomy within the Avatar architecture. IEEE →

Prior work demonstrates the individual pieces — supervisory control theory, crew-in-the-loop experiments at short delay, and autonomous traverse on Mars. The Mars Avatar Project focuses on their integration under full interplanetary latency with a specialized, coordinated fleet supervised from Earth.

Path Forward

The concept is published and open. The steps below map possible next stages — some complete, others open for collaborators.

  1. Phase 1 · Complete

    Concept & Publication

    The architecture is formalized. White Paper Version 3.6 is published on Zenodo with a permanent DOI. The open-source repository is live on GitHub.

  2. Phase 2

    Simulation

    A software simulation of the Human Operator → Mission AI → Robotic Fleet pipeline under realistic latency conditions. An open task for collaborators.

  3. Phase 3

    Prototype Testbed

    A small-scale robotic or high-fidelity software testbed to validate coordination behaviors, task allocation, and degradation responses in analog conditions.

  4. Phase 4

    Partnerships & Review

    Universities, research institutes, and aerospace organizations are invited to review the concept, challenge its assumptions, and extend it.
