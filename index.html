Independent research concept · White Paper v3.6

A reference architecture for supervising fleets of autonomous robotic avatars operating under full Earth–Mars communication latency.

A radio command needs 4 to 22 minutes to reach Mars, and the same again to report back. Nothing can be steered by hand across that gap. The Mars Avatar Project treats the delay as a fixed design condition rather than an obstacle: Earth transmits goals, and onboard autonomy determines how to reach them. The open question this work addresses is not how to move a robot, but how people stay meaningfully in command of work they cannot watch live.

§ 01.1

Key contributions

Most individual components of such a system have already been demonstrated in isolation — goal-based commanding on NASA rovers, supervisory telepresence from orbit, immersive avatar interfaces. The contribution of this work is their integration into a single specified architecture.

  1. Supervisory architecture

    An end-to-end structure in which humans set objectives and constraints, and machines determine execution — specified rather than assumed.

  2. Mission AI coordination layer

    The principal original contribution: a coordination layer between operator intent and fleet action, defined in the White Paper as a five-layer reference architecture.

  3. Multi-robot coordination

    Task allocation across a heterogeneous fleet, building on decentralised market-based methods (CBBA and successors).

  4. Human strategic oversight

    A formal goal-package protocol and graduated decision classes that keep authority for mission-level choices on Earth.

  5. Earth–Mars latency architecture

    Communication scheduling, queuing and degradation behaviour designed around the delay instead of around a hoped-for reduction of it.

  6. Open research framework

    A falsifiable central claim, measurable quality metrics, and a staged verification roadmap published openly under CC BY 4.0.

Central claim

A coordinating fleet does not remove the boundary of the modelled fault space. It converts a class of mission-loss risks into throughput losses, while leaving correlated (common-mode) failures untouched.

Stated narrowly and deliberately so that it can be tested and refuted. See §09.

§ 02

Scope of this work

The distinction below is stated early and without qualification, because the value of a concept study depends on it being read as one.

What this project is

  • A reference architecture
  • A research concept, published in full
  • An open publication with a permanent DOI
  • Independent work by a single author
  • An open invitation to review, challenge and collaborate

What this project is not

  • Flight hardware
  • Operational software
  • A NASA, ESA or other agency mission
  • A commercial product
  • A validated engineering system

No hardware has been built and no component of the architecture has been experimentally validated. References to prior work are provided for scientific context only and do not imply affiliation or endorsement.

§ 04

Architecture

Four stages separate what a human decides from what a machine executes. Mission AI is the central coordination layer: every goal that leaves Earth and every result that returns passes through it.

  1. Tier 1 · Earth

    Human operator

    Defines objectives, constraints and safety limits. Reviews returned results and authorises the next goal package. No moment-to-moment piloting occurs at any point.

  2. Tier 2 · Coordination

    Mission AI

    Translates goals into an executable, monitored plan across the fleet; maintains the shared world model; schedules communication; manages faults and degradation.

    Principal original contribution

  3. Tier 3 · Mars surface

    Robotic fleet

    Specialised avatars execute allocated tasks with local autonomy — path planning, hazard response, manipulation — on timescales of seconds, without waiting for Earth.

  4. Tier 4 · Return

    Scientific results

    Measurements, samples, imagery and telemetry are prioritised, queued and returned within the available communication windows, closing the supervisory loop.

§ 05

Mission AI

Mission AI is the principal original contribution of this work. Goal-based commanding, onboard navigation autonomy and supervisory telepresence each exist independently; what has not been specified is the layer that holds a fleet of them coherent while the operator is 4 to 22 minutes out of date. The White Paper defines it as a five-layer reference architecture with a formal goal-package protocol, graduated decision classes and measurable quality metrics.

Mission coordination

Holds the active goal set, resolves conflicts between concurrent objectives, and maintains a single authoritative plan for the fleet.

Shared world model

A common representation of terrain, assets, resources and uncertainty, kept consistent across units that observe different parts of it at different times.

Task decomposition

Expands an operator goal package into ordered, verifiable subtasks with explicit preconditions and success criteria.

Task allocation

Assigns subtasks across a heterogeneous fleet using decentralised market-based methods (CBBA and successors), tolerating intermittent links between units.

Fleet synchronisation

Keeps dependent activities aligned in time and space where two or more units must cooperate on one task.

Execution monitoring

Compares observed progress against the plan and detects divergence early enough to act locally, rather than at the next Earth window.

Fault management

Detects, isolates and contains failures; re-plans around a lost or degraded unit instead of halting the mission.

Degradation hierarchy

An ordered set of reduced-capability modes, so that the system loses throughput rather than the mission when conditions worsen.

Communication scheduling

Prioritises and queues uplink and downlink traffic against limited, intermittent windows and finite relay bandwidth.

Authority boundary: Mission AI does not set mission priorities. Which site to investigate and which sample to collect remain human decisions. The system is trusted to execute safely, not to choose objectives.

§ 06

Robotic fleet

Rather than a single general-purpose rover, the architecture assumes a small heterogeneous fleet whose units are individually simpler and collectively redundant. The composition below follows White Paper v3.6. All units are conceptual; none has been designed at hardware level.

01

Scientific Rover

Mobility · Science

Long-range traverse, in-situ measurement, sample acquisition and site characterisation ahead of and alongside other units.

02

Construction Robot

Mobility · Manipulation

Surface preparation and assembly tasks: landing pads, foundations, deployment of structures and power infrastructure.

03

Cargo Robot

Mobility · Logistics

Transport of samples, consumables, tools and components between work sites, caches and the base area.

04

Maintenance Robot

Mobility · Servicing

Inspection, cleaning, component replacement and recovery of degraded units — the fleet's means of extending its own service life.

05

Laboratory Unit

Stationary · Analysis

Fixed or slowly relocatable analysis facility for sample processing and instrumentation too heavy or too sensitive to carry on a traverse.

06

Reconnaissance Drone

Aerial · Survey

Short-duration aerial survey for route scouting, hazard identification and rapid coverage of terrain that ground units cannot reach.

§ 07

Research context

Prior work establishes each element separately: the theory of supervisory control, crew-in-the-loop operation at sub-second delay, flight-proven rover autonomy, and decentralised task allocation. The table records what each contributes and what this project proposes to add.

Comparison of previous work, its contribution, and the extension proposed by the Mars Avatar Project
Previous work Contribution Mars Avatar extension
Sheridan (1992) Telerobotics, Automation, and Human Supervisory Control. MIT Press. Formal theory of humans operating through semi-autonomous machines rather than steering them directly. Applies the operator-to-supervisor shift to a multi-agent fleet at interplanetary delay, with an explicit authority boundary.
NASA Surface Telerobotics (2013) ISS crew supervising the K10 rover at NASA Ames. First fully interactive crew operation of a planetary rover from space, using command sequencing rather than joystick control. Removes the near-real-time link assumption and generalises single-rover sequencing to fleet-level goal packages.
ESA METERON / Analog-1 (2019–2022) Astronaut operation of the Interact rover under injected delay. Force-feedback supervisory telepresence validated in lunar-analog terrain with realistic latency injection. Scales the delay by two to three orders of magnitude, where feedback-driven control is no longer available at all.
Verma et al. (2023) Perseverance AutoNav. Science Robotics. Flight evidence that onboard autonomy works at Mars: autonomy evaluated 88% of the first-Mars-year traverse. Treats proven single-vehicle autonomy as the assumed substrate and addresses the coordination layer above it.
Enhanced Autonomous Navigation (ENav) Two-stage path selection under limited onboard compute. Hazard-aware navigation demonstrated within severe flight-compute constraints. Adopted as the reference profile for per-unit local autonomy and its resource envelope.
Choi, Brunet & How (2009) Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm (CBBA) and successors. Decentralised market-based task allocation with convergence guarantees under limited communication. Used as the basis of the task-allocation layer, extended to intermittent links and heterogeneous unit capability.
ANA Avatar XPRIZE (2022) Immersive avatar systems competition. Demonstrated operator-embodiment interfaces for physical remote presence at terrestrial latency. Retains the intent of embodiment while abandoning immersion, which the signal budget makes impossible.

Where prior work demonstrates individual pieces, this project addresses their integration under full interplanetary latency. The integration itself — not any single component — is what remains unproven.

§ 08

Verification roadmap

A staged path — Earth, then Moon, then Mars — with explicit transition criteria between stages. Phases 2 onward are open; collaborators are welcome to take any of them on independently.

  1. Phase 1

    Concept and publication

    Complete

    Objective
    Formalise and publish the architecture so that it can be examined and criticised.
    Deliverables
    White Paper v3.6 archived on Zenodo with a permanent DOI; open repository on GitHub.
    Expected outcome
    A citable, falsifiable specification available for independent review.
    Potential collaborators
    Reviewers and researchers in supervisory control and planetary robotics.

  2. Phase 2

    Simulation

    Open

    Objective
    Model the goal package → coordination → execution pipeline under realistic delay and communication windows.
    Deliverables
    Open simulation environment; scenario set; measured values for the quality metrics defined in the White Paper.
    Expected outcome
    First quantitative test of the central claim — whether coordination converts mission-loss risk into throughput loss.
    Potential collaborators
    Robotics and multi-agent systems research groups; simulation and ROS communities.

  3. Phase 3

    Prototype testbed

    Open

    Objective
    Reproduce coordination behaviour on low-cost physical hardware in analog terrain with injected delay.
    Deliverables
    Small multi-unit testbed; reproducible experiment protocol; published results including negative results.
    Expected outcome
    Evidence on sim-to-real transfer and on failure modes that simulation does not surface.
    Potential collaborators
    University laboratories, student teams, maker and analog-mission communities.

  4. Phase 4

    Analog field campaign

    Open

    Objective
    Operate a heterogeneous fleet in Mars-analog terrain under full supervisory constraints for an extended period.
    Deliverables
    Campaign dataset; operator workload and throughput measurements; revised architecture specification.
    Expected outcome
    Transition criteria satisfied or refuted for progression toward flight-relevant study.
    Potential collaborators
    Analog research stations, aerospace institutes, space agencies and industry partners.

§ 09

Limitations and assumptions

Stated here rather than deferred, because a concept study that omits its own boundaries is not usable as scientific input.

  • Conceptual architecture only

    The work specifies a structure and its interfaces. It does not specify an implementation, and no implementation exists.

  • No experimental validation

    No claim in the White Paper has been tested in simulation, in hardware, or in the field. All performance figures are estimates.

  • Correlated failures remain

    Fleet coordination does not address common-mode failures. A hazard that disables one unit may disable all of them.

  • Modelled fault space is bounded

    The architecture handles faults it anticipates. Detecting the unmodelled case is stated as an open problem, not a solved one.

  • Verification of learned components

    Assuring machine-learned behaviour to flight standards is unsolved in the field generally and is not solved here.

  • Sim-to-real transfer

    Results obtained in simulation are not assumed to transfer to Martian terrain, lighting, dust or thermal conditions.

  • Hardware outside current scope

    Mass, power, thermal and radiation design for the fleet units are not addressed at any level of detail.

  • Order-of-magnitude cost

    Cost estimates are given to indicate scale only and carry no procurement or programmatic weight.
